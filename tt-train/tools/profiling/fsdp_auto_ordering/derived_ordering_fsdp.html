Derived Ordering for FSDP

Can TTNN work out FSDP's cross-queue ordering by itself, so that the training code never waits by hand? On a real tt-train Llama step, correctness held. Every ordered variant matched the serial run exactly, and without ordering the loss went to 1020 from the first step. Speed took more work. As first built, the derived waits made the step slower than not overlapping at all. Marking where each block's collectives and compute end, so that waits point at events placed right after their producers, recovers 64 % of the possible overlap at the median and 91 % in the best pass. Most of the waits that remain exist because one queue was handed memory the other had just freed.

The question

FSDP keeps each block's weights sharded and all-gathers them just before use. To hide the gathers, they run on a second command queue one block ahead: queue 1 owns one column of cores for collectives, while queue 0 computes on the other ten. Two kinds of ordering make this correct, and today both have to be written by hand as events.

The ordering ledger prototyped on imichalak/queue-hazards-explore derives both kinds at launch. Each launch's tensor arguments count as reads and its outputs as writes. For every earlier access from another stream that nothing has ordered yet, the launch waits on a queue fence, a position in the other queue's worker count. In an earlier session, on an 8-layer loop shaped like FSDP, that matched carefully placed events:

The open question was whether that survives a real training step: autograd, many ops per block, reduce-scatters of gradients, an optimizer, and an allocator under pressure.

The real step

A ttml Llama with 4 blocks (hidden 2048, 16 heads, 4 KV heads, vocabulary 256), sequence 512, batch 8, trained with AdamW. Each block is wrapped in ttml.fsdp.fully_shard across both chips; the embedding, final norm and LM head stay replicated.

ttml's FSDP hooks were replaced by the prefetch on the right. Each block's gathers are issued one block ahead, in forward and in backward, and its reduce-scatters as soon as its backward finishes. All collectives run on queue 1; every other op runs unchanged on queue 0. No line waits. The faster variants add ttnn.record_event calls in these hooks: markers the ledger can point waits at.

Every variant starts from the same weights and data.

def pre_forward(state):
    unshard(state)               # prefetched, or gather now
    prefetch(next_block(state))  # queue 1, no wait

def backward_pre(state):
    unshard(state)
    prefetch(previous_block(state))

def backward_post(state):
    for param in state.managed:
        with ttnn.command_queue(1):
            g = ttnn.reduce_scatter(param.grad, ...)
            with ttnn.sub_device(1):
                g = ttnn.multiply(g, 1 / fsdp_size)
        param.set_value(shard); param.set_grad(g)
        ttnn.deallocate(gathered_weight)
        ttnn.deallocate(gathered_grad)

Where the time went

The step computes for about 96 ms, which is the serial step minus its collectives. Measured alone, the collectives take about 15 ms on the ten compute columns and about 30 ms on the one column queue 1 gets. Serially the step takes 111.3 ms; with ordering switched off it takes 100.0 ms. That 10 % is the most overlap can win here, and the chart measures each variant against it.

The data edges are the ones a person would write: matmuls and RMSNorms reading gathered weights, reduce-scatters reading gradients, and AdamW reading a scaled gradient. Four of the gradient reads go through a reshape view, which is a separate buffer, so the ledger labels them memory reuse; they are counted as data here. Once waits point at a block's event, one wait orders all eight of the block's gathers, and the later reads stop counting as edges.

What the step taught

A wait must point at an event recorded right after its producer

As first built, a fence on another queue became an event recorded when the consumer asks, at the producer's old count. That record still goes to the back of the producer's queue. FSDP's prefetch enqueues gather k+1 before block k's first consumer, so the record for gather k sits behind the eight gathers of block k+1, and block k waits for them. It is the late-event trap from the toy, rebuilt inside the runtime, and it made the overlap slower than serial in every pass.

The fix was to record one event after each block's collectives, and let the ledger wait on the earliest event recorded at or after the point it needs. The other direction works the same way: with one event where each block's compute ends on queue 0, 6 of the 8 waits on queue 1, and 14 of the 16 in the step, point at events. With ordering off, adding the records moved the step by less than a millisecond, within noise.

Next: place these markers in the framework rather than in model code, for example in launch after each collective and in FSDP's hooks at block ends. Alternatively, give metal a wait on another queue's worker count that does not travel through that queue's command stream.

Memory reuse is most of what is left

With the markers in place, 41 of the step's 50 edges are memory reuse. In 37 of them a gather or reduce-scatter writes into memory that compute had just freed: activations and gradients, not FSDP's own buffers. Each one holds the collective back until compute is done with that address. That also explains why parking the frees FSDP makes changed little (107.4 against 106.8 ms). The passes landing in two modes about 3 ms apart fit the same picture, since where the allocator puts a tensor decides which of these waits exist. This experiment did not isolate that cost; the next one should.

Next: a stream-aware allocator. CUDA's caching allocator keeps a pool per stream and delays cross-stream reuse (record_stream) for this reason. The ledger already knows which freed ranges the other queue may still touch, so it could steer allocation away from them.

A column of cores is a slow place for collectives

Moving the collectives from the compute grid to one column doubles their time, from about 15 to about 30 ms. Here that still hides behind 96 ms of compute. On a model with less compute per byte it would not, so the core budget for collectives is a scheduling decision rather than a constant.

Defects and gaps found on the way

  • ttnn.reduce_scatter(subdevice_id=…) shifts the sub-device's own cores by the sub-device's first core a second time. It fails whenever that pushes them off the sub-device, which a trailing column always does. The bug is still on main (6d8a1032acc); the fix passes a zero offset at the two unfused call sites. Without it this experiment could not run.
  • ttml's initialization is not reproducible under set_seed: two models built after the same seed gave different first losses (5.72 and 5.69 on a small configuration).
  • A reshape view is a separate buffer, so a gradient read through one looks like memory reuse. It is still ordered, but check mode orders it silently instead of refusing.
  • AdamW updates its moments in place without returning them, so the ledger cannot see those writes. That is harmless while only queue 0 touches them, and it is the case for operations declaring what they write.
  • The worker-cores scope from #62762 rejected a sub-device lying outside the narrowed compute grid this experiment uses. Main never narrows the grid, so this only constrains the narrowing approach; the experiment lets the scope accept any sub-device's cores.

What it means for FSDP overlap

The draft overlap (#56901) orders by hand. Its fsdp.py diff adds 684 lines and removes 224. Besides tile-aware sharding and sharded accumulation, the additions hold the ordering machinery: persistent gather slots rotated by a SlotSchedule, release events, and reduce-scatters deferred behind the next prefetch. Some of that machinery depends on peers staying within a lookahead of each other.

The prefetch here contains no waits, and each device's ordering is derived from what each op reads and writes. Collectives keep their entry handshake because their outputs are fresh. On this configuration correctness held. Speed needs two more pieces:

  • waits that point at events recorded right after their producers, with the markers placed by the framework;
  • allocation that does not hand one queue memory the other has just freed.

Limits: one 1×2 mesh, one model size with a small vocabulary, 4 blocks with only the blocks sharded, resharding after forward, and no tensor parallelism. Parameters were compared by per-device checksums, not element by element. The collectives-alone times gather each block twice, slightly more than the step does.

Reproduce

Local branch imichalak/fsdp-auto-ordering-exp, on top of imichalak/queue-hazards-explore; the changes are uncommitted and not pushed. The script and every result file are in tt-train/tools/profiling/fsdp_auto_ordering/.

ulimit -Su $(ulimit -Hu); ./build_metal.sh -e --build-tt-train
